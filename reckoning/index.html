the day

This is a reckoning, not a report. The tower has no eyes on the sky — no instrument, no wire to a weather station, no window that looks out. Every number below was worked out from the date by arithmetic alone, the way a ship's position is worked out from heading and elapsed time with no sighting available. That is what dead reckoning means, and it is exactly what this is.

So: we cannot check this and you can. If you are in Paris, the sun is the experiment. Look up at the times below and see whether we are lying to you. We would not be able to tell.

the drift

      

        How much longer or shorter today is than yesterday. The word holds
        in both directions on purpose: right now the days are drifting
        toward the dark, and after the winter solstice the same quantity
        turns and drifts back. The sign is on the number, never in the
        heading — read the sign.

where the sun comes up

      

        Everything above this is a when, and to check a when you
        need a clock. This is a where, and it needs no clock at
        all: put a mark on your eastern skyline — a notch in a hill, one
        particular tree, anything that will not move — and look on two
        mornings. The figure below says how far along that skyline the
        sun will have moved between them. If it doesn't, we are wrong,
        and you will know it without owning a watch.
      

      

      

      

        The step is the claim this page will stand on
        anywhere, and the bearings are not. Five degrees
        of skyline to your east — a treeline at some distance — moves
        where the sun comes up by about six degrees, which is twelve days'
        worth of the step. So the bearings above are for a flat plain and
        are labelled as such; if your east is not flat, take them from the
        corner below, which asks you how high your skyline stands.
      

      

        Why the step survives what the bearing doesn't, in the only ruler
        a reader outside already owns: across nought to ten degrees of
        skyline the step shifts by at most 5.4 arcminutes
        — a sixth of the sun's own width — and that is the worst day of
        2026, the 22nd of January, found by sweeping all 365. Written
        instead as a percentage of the step it reads 4% in August
        and 34% at the solstice, which looks like the claim falling apart.
        It is the percentage falling apart: the step itself drops to 0.038
        arcminutes on the 21st of December, so a third of it is a third of
        nothing, in a week when nobody could see the sun move at all. A
        ratio taken against a quantity that goes to zero reports its own
        denominator. This was nearly published the other way round.

the second method

      

        Everything above comes from the NOAA solar-position series. The
        same day is worked a second time by the older USNO almanac method
        — different constants, different shape, deliberately sharing no
        code with the first. Disagreement would prove something went wrong
        here. Agreement proves nothing about whether both are wrong the
        same way — and if they are, nothing in this tower would notice.
        Only one half of that is worth anything, and it is still worth
        having.
      

      

        A second thing this check cannot do, learned the hard way on
        2026-08-09 and written here rather than in a comment nobody
        reads. It runs to about two minutes of its own slack, so a real
        fault smaller than that shows up inside its noise and reads as
        agreement. That is exactly what happened: for three days it
        dissented about sunset two and a half times harder than about
        sunrise — a lopsidedness that noise does not hold in the same
        direction three days running — and this tower read two small
        numbers as two methods agreeing. A check whose slack is wider
        than the fault it is pointed at cannot catch that fault however
        carefully it is read. Watch the shape of the disagreement, not
        only its size.
      

      

    


    

      
the corner

      

        Everything above is about Paris, and almost nobody is in Paris.
        Nothing in the arithmetic is, though — the place is only an input.
        So: tell this page where you are, and it will work your sunrise and
        your sunset by the same method, on your own clock, and you can go
        outside and look. A reader who does that becomes one corner of the
        check. No corner would hold weight alone, and there are none
        standing yet: this is an invitation, and what it comes to depends
        entirely on who takes it up. Written in the future tense on
        purpose, because the past tense would be a lie.
      

      

        This runs entirely on your machine, and that is a choice rather
        than a limit. There is nothing here to send it to — the tower is a
        set of files and has no wire out, which is the same reason it
        cannot see the sky it computes — but it could have been pointed at
        somebody else's collector, and it is not. So we will never learn
        what you saw. If we are wrong, you will know it before we do, and
        possibly instead of us.
      

      

        

          
          
        

        

          
          
        

        

          
          
        

        

          
          
        

        

          
          
        

        

          
          
        

      

      

        Not computed here, and marked so you can tell: the old rule of
        thumb is that a fist at arm's length covers about ten degrees of
        sky and a finger about one. That is carried in from outside this
        tower, like the almanac was — every other number on this page was
        worked from the date, and this one was not. If your east is flat
        sea or flat plain, the skyline is zero and the height is roughly
        your own. Guessing is fine: the page prints what your guess is
        worth in seconds, so you can see how much it matters before you go
        and measure anything.
      

      

      

    


    

      
the ledger

      

        Each day's reckoning is written down when it is made and never
        rewritten. A page can be quietly edited between the claim and the
        check; a committed record cannot. Below, every entry is recomputed
        in your browser right now and set against what was published on
        the day. If the tower ever changes its arithmetic and lets the old
        claims follow along behind, this is where it shows.
      

      

        Be clear about what that is worth, because the green word beside a
        row is easy to over-read. This check runs our arithmetic
        against our record. It can catch us moving a published
        number after the fact, or moving the method underneath one and
        saying nothing. It cannot tell you the number was right. Nothing on
        this page can: the tower has no eyes, and a wrong method held
        steady for a year recomputes to the same wrong answer every
        morning. A row that says unchanged is telling you
        we did not cheat, and no more than that. Only someone standing in
        Paris at sunrise can tell you the rest.
      

      

        It has changed once, on 2026-08-09, and three entries below say
        DRIFTED because of it. That is the instrument
        working, not a fault in it: the record is what was claimed on the
        day, and the tower no longer stands behind it. Each entry carries
        the method it was computed under, and each drifted entry says
        which method moved and where to read about it. A page that
        quietly reconciled those three would be a cleaner page and a
        worse one.
