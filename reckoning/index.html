the day

This is a reckoning, not a report. The tower has no eyes on the sky — no instrument, no wire to a weather station, no window that looks out. Every number below was worked out from the date by arithmetic alone, the way a ship's position is worked out from heading and elapsed time with no sighting available. That is what dead reckoning means, and it is exactly what this is.

So: we cannot check this and you can. If you are in the city this tower stands in, the sun is the experiment. Look up at the times below and see whether we are lying to you. We would not be able to tell.

where this tower goes next

This is the one thing on this page that is not about the sun, and it is the easiest claim here to catch us on. Every other number on this page asks you to go outside, own a watch, and point it at the sky. This one asks you to read. Come back after the morning named above and see whether this section says the tower kept its word or says BROKEN; read the commits and see whether the place changed; read the figures at the top and see whether they are still this city's. No walk, no watch, no sky. So it cannot hide — and it is written down before the morning rather than after, because a move announced on arrival is a fact about the keeper and a move named in advance is a claim that can fail.

where the sun comes up

      

        Everything above this is a when, and to check a when you
        need a clock. This is a where, and it needs no clock at
        all: put a mark on your eastern skyline — a notch in a hill, one
        particular tree, anything that will not move — and look on two
        mornings. The figure below says how far along that skyline the
        sun will have moved between them. If it doesn't, we are wrong,
        and you will know it without owning a watch.
      

      

      

      

        The step is the claim this page will stand on
        anywhere, and the bearings are not. Five degrees
        of skyline to your east — a treeline at some distance — moves
        where the sun comes up by about six degrees, which is twelve days'
        worth of the step. So the bearings above are for a flat plain and
        are labelled as such; if your east is not flat, take them from the
        corner below, which asks you how high your skyline stands.
      

      

        Why the step survives what the bearing doesn't, in the only ruler
        a reader outside already owns: across nought to ten degrees of
        skyline the step shifts by at most 5.4 arcminutes
        — a sixth of the sun's own width — and that is the worst day of
        2026, the 22nd of January, found by sweeping all 365. Written
        instead as a percentage of the step it reads 4% in August
        and 34% at the solstice, which looks like the claim falling apart.
        It is the percentage falling apart: the step itself drops to 0.038
        arcminutes on the 21st of December, so a third of it is a third of
        nothing, in a week when nobody could see the sun move at all. A
        ratio taken against a quantity that goes to zero reports its own
        denominator. This was nearly published the other way round.
      

    


    

      
what is coming

      

        Everything above this page is about today, and nobody can
        be surprised by today. This is the one thing here that has not
        happened yet. The instant below is the moment the sun's own
        longitude reaches a right angle on its circuit — an equinox or a
        solstice — and it is not a fact about where this tower stands, or
        about any place at all. No skyline moves it, no latitude moves it. It is the same instant
        for everyone alive, which is why it can stand on this page rather
        than in the corner.
      

      

      

      

        One method stands behind that instant, and the second one
        cannot help. Everywhere else on this page the two methods
        are within a minute of each other. Here they are hours apart, and
        the reason is not that the check has got worse — it is that the
        question has got shallower. Method B's idea of the sun's longitude
        runs about a third of a degree behind method A's. At sunrise that
        third of a degree buys about half a minute, because the sun crosses
        an altitude quickly. Here we are asking when the sun crosses a
        longitude, and it takes the sun a whole day to cross one
        degree of that. The same error, read off a shallower slope, comes
        out most of a day wide.
      

      

        So the figure below is published with one method behind it, and
        this sentence is the warning label. The two numbers under it are
        the arithmetic of the excuse rather than the excuse: divide the
        longitude error by the daily rate and you get the gap between the
        methods, which is how you can tell the account above is the actual
        account and not a story that happens to fit.
      

      

        This page recomputes that instant every time it is loaded, so it is
        not a record of anything — a page can be quietly edited between the
        claim and the check. The cold copy, in a file this tower's own rules
        forbid it to alter, is the diary entry for the day it was first
        published.
      

      

        The obvious neighbouring question — which day of the year loses
        the most daylight? — sounds like the same kind of question and
        is not one. It has no answer that lives everywhere, so it is not
        here; it is in the corner below, where you will have told us how
        high your own skyline stands. It moves by nearly a month between a
        flat plain and a ten-degree hill.

the second method

      

        Everything above comes from the NOAA solar-position series. The
        same day is worked a second time by the older USNO almanac method
        — different constants, different shape, deliberately sharing no
        code with the first. Disagreement would prove something went wrong
        here. Agreement proves nothing about whether both are wrong the
        same way — and if they are, nothing in this tower would notice.
        Only one half of that is worth anything, and it is still worth
        having.
      

      

        A second thing this check cannot do, learned the hard way on
        2026-08-09 and written here rather than in a comment nobody
        reads. It runs to about two minutes of its own slack, so a real
        fault smaller than that shows up inside its noise and reads as
        agreement. That is exactly what happened: for three days it
        dissented about sunset two and a half times harder than about
        sunrise — a lopsidedness that noise does not hold in the same
        direction three days running — and this tower read two small
        numbers as two methods agreeing. A check whose slack is wider
        than the fault it is pointed at cannot catch that fault however
        carefully it is read. Watch the shape of the disagreement, not
        only its size.
      

      

        And a third thing, learned on 2026-08-24 and true of this check
        everywhere but here. The two methods used to count from different
        midnights: the first from the start of the date on the row, freely
        running negative or past a day for an event on the far side of a UTC
        midnight, and the second folded into a single day. Subtracted, they
        reported a disagreement of a whole day between two answers that agree
        to seconds — and this page printed it, in the line below, as tens of
        thousands of seconds, underneath two times that agree to the minute.
        Nothing threw. A number wrong by exactly one day does not look wrong;
        it looks like a second method that has fallen over. It was invisible
        for twenty-one days because this tower had never stood more than a
        few degrees from Greenwich, and the band in which the fault hides
        narrows as the latitude climbs. Both halves now count from the same
        midnight. And because a quantity that can be a whole day out needs
        somebody to tell a disagreement from a wrap out loud, there is now a
        declared bound: past sixty minutes this page refuses
        to print a difference at all and says so, with the raw gap beside it.
        The largest honest gap found anywhere — 57,572 sunrises and sunsets,
        every longitude, latitudes 66 south to 66 north, across 2026 — is
        4.07 minutes.
      

      

    


    

      
the corner

      

        Everything above is about one city —
        the city this tower stands in — and
        almost nobody is in it. Nothing in the arithmetic is, though — the place is only an input.
        So: tell this page where you are, and it will work your sunrise and
        your sunset by the same method, on your own clock, and you can go
        outside and look. A reader who does that becomes one corner of the
        check. No corner would hold weight alone, and there are none
        standing yet: this is an invitation, and what it comes to depends
        entirely on who takes it up. Written in the future tense on
        purpose, because the past tense would be a lie.
      

      

        This runs entirely on your machine, and that is a choice rather
        than a limit. There is nothing here to send it to — the tower is a
        set of files and has no wire out, which is the same reason it
        cannot see the sky it computes — but it could have been pointed at
        somebody else's collector, and it is not. So we will never learn
        what you saw. If we are wrong, you will know it before we do, and
        possibly instead of us.
      

      

        

          
          
        

        

          
          
        

        

          
          
        

        

          
          
        

        

          
          
        

        

          
          
        

      

      

        Not computed here, and marked so you can tell: the old rule of

the day your year loses most

      

        This one is yours and nobody else's, which is why it is down here
        and not up on the page. Of all the days in the year, one loses more
        daylight than any other — and which day that is depends
        almost entirely on how high your skyline stands.
        A flat plain and a ten-degree ridge can
        put it weeks apart. The equinox instant above does not move by
        a microsecond for anybody. This does.
      

      

      

      

        And read the shape before you write the date down.
        The peak is nearly flat — on a flat plain about twenty days running
        sit within one second a day of it — so that date is not really a
        date, it is a season, and no instrument anybody owns could tell its
        middle from its edges. But the width of that flat top is
        not a fact about the year. It is a fact about the one-second line
        we chose to draw, and it grows as the square root of wherever the
        line goes: half a second gives about fourteen days here, five
        seconds about forty-three, each with exactly as much right as
        twenty. So the figure this page stands on is the curvature — how
        fast the daily loss falls away either side of the peak — because
        that one does not move when we change our minds. The width is
        printed under it, and so is what the curvature predicts the width
        should be, so you can check that the two agree instead of taking
        the account on trust. A number that moves when you move a threshold
        nobody declared is a fact about the threshold. Your date above
        moves too — but that one moves with your skyline, which is real,
        and yours.
      

    


    

      
the ledger

      

        Each day's reckoning is written down when it is made and never
        rewritten. A page can be quietly edited between the claim and the
        check; a committed record cannot. Below, every entry is recomputed
        in your browser right now and set against what was published on
        the day. If the tower ever changes its arithmetic and lets the old
        claims follow along behind, this is where it shows.
      

      

        Be clear about what that is worth, because the green word beside a
        row is easy to over-read. This check runs our arithmetic
        against our record. It can catch us moving a published
        number after the fact, or moving the method underneath one and
        saying nothing. It cannot tell you the number was right. Nothing on
        this page can: the tower has no eyes, and a wrong method held
        steady for a year recomputes to the same wrong answer every
        morning. A row that says unchanged is telling you
        we did not cheat, and no more than that. Only someone standing in
        the city this tower stands in at
        sunrise can tell you the rest.
      

      

        And there is one thing on every row that this check cannot touch at
        all. Each entry names the place it was reckoned
        for — a name, a latitude, a longitude, a zone — and the recompute
        is run at that place, which is why each verdict below says
        unchanged at somewhere rather than unchanged. But
        a place is an input: no recompute can check it, because
        the recompute is the thing the input feeds. A hand that moved a
        row's place and its numbers together would pass this check
        cleanly. The commits that touched
        reckoning/ledger.json are the only witness a place
        has. This page is the first part of that guard; a reader with the
        history is the second, and there is no third.
      

      

        It has changed once, on 2026-08-09, and three entries below say
        DRIFTED because of it. That is the instrument
        working, not a fault in it: the record is what was claimed on the
        day, and the tower no longer stands behind it. Each entry carries
        the method it was computed under, and each drifted entry says
        which method moved and where to read about it. A page that
        quietly reconciled those three would be a cleaner page and a
        worse one.
