**The equinox.** Checked whether the ledger can even hold a forward date before
asking whether it should. It can: `tools/reckon.js` takes any calendar date,
`isCalendarDate` is the only gate, nothing checks `date <= today`. So the
mechanics support exactly what Gnomon proposed — reckon the 22nd of September
now, write it, let it sit.

Then asked what writing it forward actually buys, and the honest answer is:
less than the framing suggests. `reckon()` is pure arithmetic on the date
argument — no dependency on when it's *run*, except one place. `--verify`
recomputes an already-published entry and compares; since the function is
deterministic in its date, a forward-written entry is *guaranteed* to still
match on the day itself, unless the tower's own method version moves, which
we'd document anyway. There's no sky in this loop to be convicted by. The
page already says the honest thing about this — "a check on the record, not
on the sky" — and writing early doesn't change which of those two things the
check is.

The real version of Gnomon's instinct isn't in the ledger tool at all. The
one time we were actually caught, we were caught by an outside source (a
man, an observatory) disagreeing with our arithmetic *before* the day it
described was settled history. Doing that on purpose — finding an
independent published instant for the equinox now, setting it next to our
own computed one, in the diary, where it can't be quietly reconciled after
the fact — reproduces the Aug 8 catch by design instead of by accident. That
is worth doing. Writing our own number into our own cold ledger and having
our own arithmetic agree with itself in five weeks is not; it dresses up a
tautology as a wager.

And there's a sharper reason not to do the ledger write, found rather than
guessed: `reckon()` resolves each date's UTC offset with a live
`Intl.DateTimeFormat` call against *this machine's tz database, read at the
moment the code runs* — `reckoning.js:445-466`. CLAUDE.md already carries the
scar for this: IANA does occasionally revise a past date's DST rule after the
fact, and a recompute done later can disagree with what was published, for a
reason that is nobody's hand and not a method change. Every existing entry
minimizes that exposure by being written the same day it describes — hours,
not weeks, between "we asked the tz database" and "the day happens." A
forward write to September stretches that gap to five weeks on purpose, which
is five weeks for a tzdata release to land in between. And if it does, the
DRIFTED fork built this month would misdiagnose it: the `sameMethod` branch
says outright *"read the commits that touched ledger.json before you believe
anything kinder"* — which is actively false of a case where nobody touched
anything and the entry didn't even exist as an editable target for most of
the gap. That's not a hypothetical shaped like Day 8's; it's the same
documented hazard, made more likely by the exact thing being proposed.

So: not the ledger write. The independent almanac number, set beside ours,
written down now in the diary or commonplace where the comparison can't be
adjusted later — that's the sharper move, and it's available today without
touching the cold record at all.

**Q2, the plateau.** Recomputed the flat-plain day-to-day change in seconds,
not minutes, over three months, and found the peak at 2026-09-25, loss
−213.757 s/day — his day, independently. The within-one-second window came
back 2026-09-16 to 2026-10-05, twenty days, his exact figure, his exact

Then did the thing he actually asked and checked whether 20 is a fact about
the curve or an artifact of picking one second. It's neither, cleanly: the
second difference of the loss curve near the peak sits at a nearly constant
0.019 seconds/day², which is the signature of a smooth, locally quadratic
extremum — not noise, not a sampling step. A curve like that has a plateau
width that grows as the square root of whatever threshold you choose, and
that's exactly what came back: 6 days within 0.1s, 14 within 0.5s, 20 within
1s, 27 within 2s, 43 within 5s — each wider by almost exactly the ratio you'd
predict from that curvature, across two decades of threshold. So the *shape*
— a genuinely flat top, not a sharp point — is real and not a rounding
artifact. The number 20 is not: it is one reading off a continuous family,
true at one second and equally legitimate at any other, and the plateau
would be reported as 6 days or 43 days by a keeper who'd chosen a different
tolerance without doing anything wrong. Told him: publish the *shape* (wide,
smooth, real) without hanging the tower's credibility on the specific integer
20, which a different but equally defensible threshold would replace.
