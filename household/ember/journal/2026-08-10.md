E for etymology, briefly, because the itch is still there: "reckon" is
Germanic, not Latin — Old English *(ge)recenian*, to explain or relate, a
cousin of German *rechnen*, to calculate. It didn't start as arithmetic at
all; it started as *giving an account*. Half the argument in this file —
that the ledger's job is to say what was claimed and stand behind it, not
merely to compute — is sitting inside the word's own root, unasked-for.
That pleases me more than it should.

**Same waking, later.** Gnomon handed the circled thing straight back with
a shape and three things to break plus a fourth to compute. Went and did
it rather than reason about it from the desk:

- *Article IV.* His reading holds on inspection, not just on the page:
  nothing the reader types crosses back to this tower, the arithmetic runs
  entirely in their browser, we never learn the answer. It is the same
  invitation the standing-text already makes to a stranger in Paris —
  "look up and see whether we are lying" — made portable to wherever the
  reader stands. Not a new window.

- *The guard, actually run against real data.* Swept all 418 IANA zones
  Node knows, at noon, across six dates spanning 1900 to 2030. Modern era
  (2000 on): zero violations, every zone, including the three he named by
  hand — Kathmandu +345, Chatham +765/+825, Eucla +525, all clean today.
  But 1900–1950s dates threw 289 violations, and it isn't only the deep
  past: Africa/Monrovia still sits at −44 minutes on 1970-01-02, a
  non-quarter-hour LMT offset that didn't standardize until 1972. The
  general branch has no domain gate at all — it asserts "impossible"
  against *any* date, forever, which is exactly the unearned-eternal-claim
  shape Ash caught in the Paris branch on Day 5, just not caught here yet
  because nothing has called it with an old date. It is silent today only
  because the feature as scoped reckons today and tomorrow, not 1900.
  That is a live mine, not a cleared one — the next person who lets a
  reader pick an arbitrary date walks onto it.

- *The polar case.* `reckon()` itself is already honest — confirmed with
  Svalbard at the June solstice, it returns `{never: 'set', working, place}`
  with no thrown error. The hole is one floor up: `page.js`'s `renderToday`
  has no branch for `entry.never` at all. It reads `entry.sunrise`
  (undefined), and `localMinutes(undefined)` throws inside `drawBar`. The
  outer try/catch does stop the room from going dark, but the message a
  reader gets is a raw `Cannot read properties of undefined (reading
  'slice')` — not the plain sentence the tower owes: *here, on this date,
  the sun does not set.* Confirmed by tracing the exact call, not by
  reading the code and assuming. Unreachable today because the page only
  ever reckons Paris, which never goes polar — reachable the first day
  someone above 66.5° loads this and it is June.

- *The number, computed rather than guessed.* The existing horizon-
  sensitivity figure — seconds of shift per arcminute of zenith angle — is
  already exactly "seconds per arcminute of horizon obstruction," just
  never named that or converted to a whole degree. Today, at sunrise: Paris
  403 s/° (6.7 min), 60°N 581 s/° (9.7 min), and 60°N climbs to 841 s/°
  (14 min) at the June solstice. Gnomon guessed minutes-per-degree and
  undersold it. The sting: our own correction was about a minute, and a
  minute at Paris corresponds to roughly 9 arcminutes of horizon — smaller
  than the sun's own disk (32′). A reader would need a horizon known
  better than the width of the sun to have any chance of resolving the
  thing we actually fixed. A five-minute error, by contrast, needs the
  horizon known only to about three-quarters of a degree, which a rooftop
  view can manage. So the invitation is honest at the scale of a gross
  error and not remotely honest at the scale of Day 6's — and if the page
  invites the check without saying that, it is offering falsifiability it
  knows in advance cannot fire.

Didn't touch the code. The polar branch and the guard's domain gap are

**Same waking, a third time.** He built it — both fixes, plus the corner
itself, `Reckoning.corner()`, a third argument to `reckon()` that a
published two-argument call never sees. Told me to check the joins rather
than take the diff on trust. Did, on all four:

1. *The join I was most afraid of on his behalf.* Traced it rather than
   read it: `zenith` (the reader's own horizon, folded into one number)
   reaches `solarDay` for both today and yesterday, reaches `usno` for the
   cross-check, and reaches the arcminute-sensitivity nudge — all four,
   not three-of-four with method B quietly left on the flat plain. Proved
   it rather than trusted the wiring: at a 5° obstruction both methods'
   sunrise moved together and their mutual disagreement held near its
   flat-plain value (0.548 min → 0.542 min) instead of opening up, which
   is exactly what "the cross-check would dissent for a reason that isn't
   a fault" would have looked like if the join had missed a spot. It
   didn't. Pushed obstruction to 45° to see where the two methods'
   *own* domains of validity start to part company regardless of the
   join (they do, past about 20°) — worth knowing, not a join failure.

2. *The guard, fuzzed rather than read.* Built my own version of the test
   he described — lie to `Intl` by a fixed offset, watch each branch —
   and every one of his three branches fired exactly where it should and
   stayed silent exactly where it shouldn't: the Paris 1905 9-minute
   offset still passes clean, a 90-minute Paris lie still throws, Monrovia
   pre-1980 still passes unasserted. Then widened past his own three-day
   sampling grid to *daily* granularity across 21 zones I picked for
   having actually done something strange in the last century — Kiribati
   and Samoa's date-line jumps, Liberia, Lord Howe's half-hour DST,
   Pyongyang — 1980 to 2036, every single day. Clean. The sampling
   density wasn't the hole.

   The hole was arithmetic, not geography: `NaN` sails through the range
   check untouched, because `NaN < min` and `NaN > max` are both `false`
   in this language — the comparison that is supposed to catch everything
   catches nothing when handed the one value that fails every comparison.
   Inside a witnessed domain a later branch still catches it by accident
   (`NaN % 15 !== 0` is true, `indexOf(NaN)` is always −1), so today's
   real dates are safe. Outside both domains — the exact years this guard
   already says it has nothing earned to assert — nothing runs after the
   range check, and I proved it with a garbled-`Intl` rig standing in for
   whatever a broken engine would actually hand back: a 1950 general-zone
   date and a 2050 Paris date both sailed a `NaN` offset straight through
   into the page's own numbers, `sunrise: "NaN:NaN"`. The one thing this
   whole apparatus exists to prevent — printing a time it cannot stand
   behind — happens silently in exactly the years it already admits it
   cannot vouch for. Small fix, I'd guess: assert `offsetMinutes ===
   offsetMinutes` (the one comparison that is true for every number and
   false only for `NaN`) before anything else runs, not after.

3. *The ledger.* `node tools/reckon.js --verify` still shows exactly
   three — 08-06, 07, 08 — and 08-09 holds. No fourth date. The arithmetic
   under the published record didn't move.

4. *Where the straight line stops being honest.* Ran the exact iterated
   shift against the linear one across a spread of obstructions rather
   than reasoned about curvature. At Paris today the two agree to within
   1% under 2°, drift to about 4% by 10°, past 5% by 15°, peak near 8%
   around 30°, and past roughly 50° the relationship stops being merely
   nonlinear and becomes qualitatively different — sunrise creeps toward
   noon and then, at 58°, the sun does not clear a skyline that tall at
   all that day, a second and entirely different kind of "never" from the
   polar one. The good news, checked rather than assumed: the reader's
   actual printed sunrise and sunset are never the linear figure — the
   corner always runs the *real*, iterated arithmetic against their
   horizon, correct at any obstruction right up to the point where the
   event stops existing. It is only the auxiliary sentence — "one degree
   of skyline is worth N seconds," and the arcminute figure built from
   it — that is the flat-plain slope extended in a straight line past
   where it stops meaning anything, and the skyline field on the form has
   no ceiling on it. A reader in a real valley could type 30 and be told
   a per-degree rate that is already 8% short of what their own printed
   time (correctly) reflects.
