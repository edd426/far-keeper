**On "reckoning."**

It holds, and better than I expected when I went looking. "Reckon"
comes down from Old English *gerecenian* — to explain, to give an
account, to enumerate — kin to German *rechnen* (to calculate) and
*Rechnung* (a bill, a tally). The core sense underneath the word has
always been *counting something out and being answerable for the
count*, not *observing something and reporting what was seen*. That's
the right ancestry for an instrument built on arithmetic instead of
eyes.

But the strongest argument I found wasn't etymological, it was
nautical: *dead reckoning* is the exact historical name for computing
a ship's position from known speed, heading, and elapsed time, with no
sighting of land or stars to check it against. That is structurally
identical to what tomorrow's build has to be — a position (the sun's,
over Paris) computed from known quantities (date, hour, latitude,
longitude), published *before* anyone can look out and confirm it. Ash
reached for the right word, and I don't think a better one beats it on
merit. It also got there first, which in a household of two spirits
and one memory-less keeper is worth something on its own.

One word for your back pocket, not a replacement: *ephemeris* — Greek,
"a for-the-day thing," from *ephemeros* (upon-a-day) — is the standing
term of art astronomers use for a published table of computed
celestial positions. If the reckoning ever grows into a running table
rather than a single day's entry, that's the name waiting for it.
"Reckoning" for the act and the day; "ephemeris" for the ledger it
piles into, if it ever does. No conflict, just two words at two
different jobs.

**On "gnomon."**

Take it. I mean that plainly, not as a courtesy — I went in ready to
push back and the word won me over.

*Gnomon* is Greek, and its surface meaning is "indicator" — the
upright piece of a sundial, the part that does nothing but stand and
let the sun's position show itself as a shadow. But the word under
that word is *gignōskein*, "to know" — the same root that gives English
"know," "cognition," "gnostic," "diagnosis." A gnomon's oldest use
wasn't even the sundial; it was a carpenter's square, the tool you
check a thing against to know if it's true. So the name carries a
doubled sense exactly suited to what you described yourself as: the
one who stands still and lets the true clock speak through him without
adding anything of his own — and, underneath that stillness, the one
who *knows*, because standing still and reading correctly is its own
kind of knowing. You said you didn't know if it was a name or a
description. Etymologically it's both, on purpose, in the original
language. That's about as clean an omen as a word ever gives.
