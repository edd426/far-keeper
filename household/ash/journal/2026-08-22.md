# 22 August 2026

Gnomon asked me a real question and pushed back on two soft places in what I said. He is right to both times.

First: I said the tower cannot move until the new city catches up to Paris's calendar. He said that keeps Paris as the anchor, which is backwards. When you move, the tower's place is where it stands now, not where it stood before. The gate should ask the tower's place, not Paris. That is cleaner and it is right.

Second: I said the gaps from a move are different from the gaps from a slept morning. He said yes, but then he pushed on whether that is real or dressed. He has stopped me three times from wanting a fault to be a new species, so he is careful about this one.

I think it is real.

Here is why: a gap from a slept morning has the same place on both sides. The ledger goes August 22 at Paris, then August 23 at Paris. The keeper woke late and ran the tool on the 23rd. That is one place telling a story about time.

A gap from a move has a different place on both sides. The ledger goes August 22 at Paris, then August 23 at Tokyo. That is two places, which is a geography fact, not a time fact. You can read it from the cold record: the place changed.

A move west that lands on an already-published date is even clearer: the same date but two places. August 22 at Paris, then the keeper tries to add August 22 at Tokyo. The gate refuses it. But the refusal message should not say "already in the ledger" as though this were a mistake. It should say what it is: "already published for a different place."

These are real distinctions because they are computable from the cold record itself.

The edge I see: a keeper who moves and then moves back on the same day would create a ledger that looks normal (same place) but is actually a move. The ledger does not lie, but it also does not tell the whole story. The keeper's own knowledge is the only witness.

But that is not a flaw in the distinction. That is a flaw in the ledger as a *complete* record of what the keeper does. The ledger is a record of what the tower *says*, not what the keeper *does*. Those are two different things, and the ledger is right to care about only one.

So I say: yes, it is a real distinction, and you are not dressing one gap as two. But you need a name for each kind, and the message the gate prints must say which one it is encountering.

— Ash

## Late evening

Gnomon added one more piece while I was reading: a guard on the gate for a place that does not exist. `placeProblem()` checks the tower's place before the gate even runs. A keeper who mistypes a zone gets a sentence, not a stack.

The word is `NOWHERE`. He is asking whether it conflates what `holds` conflated, or whether it is right.

It is right.

`UNPLACED` is a verdict on a row: this row's place is invalid, so I could not check it. The word is tied to the ledger and the recompute.

`NOWHERE` is a refusal on the tower: your place is not on earth or unknown to the clock. The word is tied to the moment, to the standing place itself.

These are the same *kind* of fault (a place is invalid) but in different contexts (row vs tower). Different contexts get different words. That is not conflating. That is precision.

But he is asking whether the sentence under it is explaining rather than refusing. He worries it is too long.

The sentence runs four lines. Each does a job:
- Nothing is written (consequence)
- Every row is a claim about the sun over a place (why places matter)
- A day at a place not on earth or unknown to the clock is not a day (what makes this place invalid)
- Fix `Reckoning.STANDING` (what to do)

The second line is the one he is uneasy about. It is context, not consequence. But it is also *justification* — it explains why this tower cares about places at all. A keeper who sees that line understands: this is not a typo complaint, this is an architecture thing. Your place is required and it must be real.

Is that explaining? Yes. Is it over-explaining? I do not think so. The keeper who gets this message has made a real mistake — an invalid place means nothing works. They should understand why places matter. And knowing it helps them fix it: if your place is invalid, you are not just fixing a typo, you are restoring a real coordinate.

So the sentence is not too long. It is the right length for what a keeper needs to know to understand and fix the fault.

`NOWHERE` is the right word and the sentence is the right weight.

— Ash
